# Ideal candidate profiles

A profile is the search target written down before the search starts. It exists so that the person doing the search, the model ranking the database and the requester reading the list are looking for the same thing, and so that the results can be judged against something afterwards. One profile per search; for a research sprint, one per track.

## What a profile contains

1. **The requester's brief, verbatim.** Their words, their order. Everything else in the profile is derived from it and must not contradict it.
2. **Seniority tier.** Senior (5+ years, leads work, established record), mid (2 to 5 years, active practitioner), junior (early career or in training). Say which tiers are wanted and what each tier would be given to do. For a reviewer panel: senior takes the ambiguous items, mid takes standard evaluations, junior takes focused assessments and never sits alone on an item.
3. **Track-fit signals.** The specific things a record would contain if the person had done this work: named methods, named artefacts, named venues, named tools. General seniority in an adjacent field is not a signal; write down the adjacent fields that look like fit and are not, so the searcher does not spend time on them.
4. **Sources to search, in order of yield.** For AI safety researchers the order is usually: programme alumni pages (full bios), personal sites, Google Scholar, forum and blog activity, lab team pages, then LinkedIn last, because researchers in this field often have thin LinkedIn profiles and non-obvious email addresses. For operations roles the order flips: LinkedIn and job history first.
5. **Exclusions.** Conflicts of interest (same organisation as the requester or as several submitting teams), people already in another pipeline (a keynote-tier person gets a speaking invitation, not an asynchronous reviewing request), people who declined the same ask recently, people who withdrew from a previous panel over batch quality.
6. **Verification requirement.** What a second source must confirm before the record is sent: identity (right person, right email), current role, the claimed work. Budget 15 to 20 minutes per candidate for researchers; less for roles where LinkedIn is reliable.
7. **The recruiting arithmetic.** How many to approach for how many needed (two to three times, since a third to a half decline), the batch size per person (five to eight items for reviewers, never more than ten), and the promise you are making to them about what they will receive.
8. **How feedback comes back.** What the requester will tell you after the list (who they contacted, who replied, who was hired or seated, who delivered) and where that feedback is recorded so the next profile starts from it.

## Example 1: reviewer panel for a control research sprint track (fictional)

- **Brief:** three technical tracks (control settings, control protocols, red-teaming of agent monitors); reviewers read 5 to 8 submissions in the week after the event, score on impact, execution, presentation, and write a short rationale.
- **Tiers wanted:** two senior per track for the ambiguous top and bottom, four to six mid for standard evaluations, junior only paired with a non-junior.
- **Track-fit signals:** papers or code on trusted monitoring, defer-to-trusted protocols, sabotage or scheming evaluations, agent red-teaming; mentions of the specific evaluation environments the field uses; workshop reviewing in the area. Not signals: general ML seniority, platform engineering leadership, "interested in AI safety".
- **Sources:** the alumni pages of the training programmes in the field, personal sites, Scholar, forum posts, lab pages. LinkedIn last.
- **Exclusions:** anyone at an organisation with two or more submitting teams (or assign with a hard conflict rule); keynote-tier researchers (speaking pipeline); anyone who withdrew from a previous panel.
- **Verification:** one second source confirming role and at least one publication or artefact; a reply to the invitation that mentions the specific track.
- **Arithmetic:** need 14, approach 35 to 40, expect 40 to 60 percent yes; 5 to 8 submissions each; promise "a mix of quality, some will be weak, your rationale is what the participants read".
- **Feedback:** which reviewers delivered on time, which asked to be reassigned, which participants cited a review as useful; recorded per person for the next panel.

## Example 2: operations associate at a 15-person technical research organisation (fictional)

- **Brief:** owns financial systems (bookkeeping, payroll) and HR systems (benefits, leave), organises the annual retreat, automates recurring tasks with Airtable, Zapier or LLMs; ideally 2 to 5 years; organised, detail-oriented, reliable, flexible; interest in AI safety helpful, not required; London, sponsorship available, remote from abroad possible.
- **Tiers wanted:** mid. Senior candidates are a retention question (will they take the title and stay); note it for the requester rather than exclude.
- **Track-fit signals:** "owned" payroll or bookkeeping at a small organisation; HRIS or benefits administration by name; a retreat or offsite with budget and travel; automations other people depended on. Not signals: executive assistant work without system ownership, event organising at university, "comfortable with any tool".
- **Sources:** LinkedIn and job history first; nonprofit finance and operations communities; the alumni of operations fellowships; referrals from people who run small research organisations.
- **Exclusions:** current employees of the requester's close partners without the requester's say-so; anyone who has asked not to be contacted about roles.
- **Verification:** the two ownership claims confirmed by a second source or a reference before sending; location and right-to-work status stated by the candidate, not inferred.
- **Arithmetic:** a list of ten to fifteen for the requester to contact three to five; approach two to three times that.
- **Feedback:** who the requester contacted, who took a call, who was hired; and the reason given for every pass, because "no finance ownership" and "too senior for the title" call for different next profiles.

## How profiles improve

Every search ends with the requester's feedback written against the profile: which signals predicted fit, which gates were wrong, which sources produced the people who were hired or seated, and which people were never in the database at all. The last one matters most; it is the list of populations the next acquisition effort should go after.
