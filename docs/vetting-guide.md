# Vetting guide

Fifteen to twenty minutes per candidate, against four questions. Written for judges of research submissions; the same checklist works for speakers and mentors with the load section swapped out.

## The four questions

1. **Track fit.** Does their work match the sub-area they would assess? General technical seniority is not fit. A principal engineer who has never read a paper in the sub-area cannot evaluate research in it, and the participants will notice. Rate on the sub-area, not the title.
2. **Availability.** The work falls in a specific window (for judges, the week after the event, two to three hours). Someone at two conferences that week is a decline waiting to happen.
3. **Topic understanding.** From their own words in the first reply: do they describe the event's focus the way we would, or something adjacent? "Physics meets AI safety" for a governance event is a mismatch, not a bonus.
4. **Verification.** Is this the person we think it is, and does the address reach them? See below.

## Seniority tiers and what they get assigned

| Tier | Typical profile | What to assign |
|---|---|---|
| Senior | 5+ years, leads a group or has an established publication record | The ambiguous and borderline submissions; one senior track-fit reviewer on every project |
| Mid | 2 to 5 years, active researcher with a specialty | Standard technical evaluations in their sub-area |
| Junior | PhD student or early career, specific technical skills | Focused technical checks, never as the only reviewer on a project |

Load: five to eight projects per judge, two to three hours in total, absolute ceiling ten. Past seven the quality of the notes drops.

## Where to look, because the obvious profile is often empty

Researchers in this field often have a thin public profile: a bare headline, few connections, no summary, a personal address. Do not dismiss on that. Check, in this order:

1. The alumni page of the training programme they came through (full bios, research descriptions).
2. A personal website (CV, research statement).
3. Community forums where research is discussed (active participation is an expertise signal).
4. A scholar profile (publications even when the profile does not list them).
5. The team page of their lab or organisation.

Two things not to read as evidence when the profile comes through a third-party data provider: the "about" paragraph, which arrives truncated, and the skills list, which is auto-extracted keywords. Everything else in such a record is complete. If someone sends a screenshot of the profile taken while logged in, that beats the provider's copy for the "about" section.

## Red flags

Immediate:
- Topic confusion about the event's focus.
- Visa or CV motivation as the stated reason for joining.
- No verifiable expertise in the sub-area.
- Very junior with no evaluation experience.
- Generic interest ("I like AI").

Worth a note:
- Over-committed that week.
- Slow to reply (seven days or more) before they have committed to anything.
- Same organisation as several participating teams (conflict of interest at assignment time).

Minor:
- Twelve-plus hours of timezone difference.
- No prior judging experience (a briefing fixes this).

## Batch vetting (eight or more candidates at once)

1. Read every intro first. Put name, address, profile link and self-described background in one table before judging anyone.
2. Check every profile, then run one web search per person for their name plus the sub-area. This catches publications, talks and posts the profile misses.
3. Rate each: **strong** (direct experience in the sub-area, published or at a relevant lab), **moderate** (adjacent field, could assess some tracks), **weak** (no connection to the sub-area, whatever the seniority), **unable to vet** (no public presence; ask for a CV).
4. Present the table to whoever decides, with a one-line justification per person.
5. Decide before any confirmation goes out. This gate is load-bearing in one direction: a confirmation cannot be undone (see docs/outreach.md), a decline can always be softened later. When an unvetted candidate presses for an answer, hold rather than confirm.

## Third-party referrers

When a newsletter operator, recruiter or partner offers to source candidates: accept the help, set the bar in one sentence early ("direct experience in X, Y or Z"), vet every referral with the batch process above, and decline gracefully by attributing the decision to the organising side rather than to the referrer. Keep the relationship warm; the need recurs and topics change. Add every referred person to the candidates table, declined or not: they are contacts now, with the referrer recorded in `source_ref`.
